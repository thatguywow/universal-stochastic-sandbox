Read this before trusting a number

This engine returns what you feed it. Run ten million draws at p = 0.322 and it reports 0.322 with a very tight interval — but that interval describes the random number generator, not the world. Monte Carlo sampling adds no information that was not already in your parameters.

Everything below follows from that one fact. If you take away nothing else: the narrow interval is almost never the one to quote.

Where the counts come from

“How many yes” and “out of how many” are the only real information in the pipeline. The engine cannot supply them.

  1. Count them. Define the population first. “61 of 152 people on this street on Tuesday” is a real measurement of that population — it becomes wrong when you call it “the city”.
  2. Query data you already have. count(*) FILTER (WHERE x) and count(*).
  3. Cite a study that reports both a percentage and its sample size. No sample size means it is an assumption, not evidence.
  4. You have nothing. Do not invent counts. Report an upper bound instead, or work out what value would change your decision.

Reading the warnings

Amber notes are advisory. Red ones mean the number above them is not what it appears to be — always read those.

WarningWhat to do
parameter uncertainty exceeds Monte Carlo error Normal and expected. Do not raise sample size; get more data.
quantised by the inner simulation grid Your event is rarer than the inner run can resolve. Raise replications, or report a bound.
zero observations The central value is the prior, not data. Quote the upper end.
counts are overdispersed Not Poisson. The exact interval shown is too narrow.
ranking is not resolved The top inputs overlap. Do not act on the order; raise precision.

Method notes

  • Sampling. Vectorized inverse-transform: U ~ Uniform(0,1) mapped through F-1(U). Poisson uses a saturating CDF table with searchsorted, verified bit-identical to SciPy and ~120× faster.
  • Intervals. Wilson score for proportions, Garwood (exact Poisson) for rates, Student-t for means. Normal approximations are not used where they are invalid — at p=0.002, n=500 measured coverage is Wilson 0.913 vs Wald 0.639.
  • Posteriors. Beta–Binomial, Gamma–Poisson, and a Student-t marginal for a mean with unknown variance (treating an estimated σ as known under-covers: 92% vs 95% at n=8).
  • Variance reduction. Antithetic standard errors are computed across pairs, never by pooling — pooling overstates error by ~3.4×. Importance sampling weights are formed in log space and flagged when the effective sample size degenerates.
  • Sensitivity. Saltelli design, Sobol'-Mauntz for first-order and Jansen for total effect, with bootstrap confidence intervals — a bare index would repeat the very mistake this tool exists to prevent. Model determinism is tested directly.
  • Goodness of fit. KS p-values use a parametric bootstrap. The textbook test with fitted parameters is invalid: on genuinely normal data it rejected 0.0% of the time against a nominal 5%.
  • MCMC. Multiple dispersed chains with split R-hat and autocorrelation-adjusted effective sample size; non-convergence is reported rather than hidden.

315 tests. Samplers are checked against analytic truth and intervals against measured coverage, not merely for running without error.
